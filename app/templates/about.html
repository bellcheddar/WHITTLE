About and methods

What WHITTLE does, in plain terms

A nanobody is the smallest piece of an antibody that still binds something on its own. Camelids make them naturally, they are about a tenth the size of a normal antibody, and they are easy to produce, which is why they turn up in diagnostics, imaging and increasingly in medicines.

Designing one is now cheap. Testing one is not. A single candidate has to be turned into DNA, expressed in cells, purified and measured, and that is days of work and real money for each one. So when a design campaign hands you 500 candidates per target, the expensive question is not which one is best. It is which twenty or so are worth spending the money on.

WHITTLE answers that question for 2,500 candidates against five targets, 500 each, and narrows them to 24 per target. It does this by running the cheap checks on everything and the expensive checks only on what survives. Each candidate is judged on four things:

The four axes in plain terms
AxisThe question it asksConfidence
Binding Does it look like it will stick to the thing it was designed for? weak
Non-specific binding Will it also stick to everything else it meets? mixed
Thermostability Will it survive being warmed, frozen, shipped and stored? strongest
Expression Can it actually be made, and will it stay dissolved? untested

Those confidence ratings are not opinions. Each axis is scored for 250 deliberately broken sequences as well as for the real candidates: the control takes a real candidate and shuffles the order of its three binding loops, which should make it worse at binding while leaving its length, its amino-acid makeup and its scaffold untouched. An axis that cannot tell a real candidate from its own shuffled twin is not measuring anything, whatever it looks like.

  • Thermostability earns the top rating. The real candidate scores above its own shuffled twin in 210 of 250 pairs, and every one of the five targets clears that test on its own.
  • Non-specific binding is mixed, and worst where it matters most. It separates real from shuffled on PRLR, is too weak to call on GLP1R and TL1A, and does nothing at all on EGFR and TNFa. The site marks the axis per target rather than once overall, because the answer genuinely differs between them.
  • Expression cannot be tested this way at all, and is marked untested rather than good or bad. Two thirds of what it reads is amino-acid makeup, which the shuffle leaves identical, so the control cannot move it. Nor would a sharper synthetic control settle it: the two main components are defined from amino-acid makeup, so a deliberately insoluble sequence scores badly by arithmetic rather than by evidence. Settling it needs laboratory expression or solubility measurements on real nanobodies, and there are none in this control set.
  • Binding waits on the co-folding tier, and is calibrated the same way once it lands: against scrambled sequences co-folded through exactly the same pipeline.

The honest framing is that WHITTLE is good at throwing things away and only suggestive about ranking. A candidate at the bottom of a target's list has something specific and identifiable wrong with it. A candidate at the top has nothing obviously wrong with it, which is a weaker statement and a different one.

How to read the numbers

  • Nothing here is measured. Every value on this site is predicted or estimated from the sequence, or from a structure that was itself predicted. No molecule described here has been made.
  • Scores are percentiles within one target. A score of 90 means "better than 90 per cent of the other candidates against this same target". It does not mean 90 per cent of anything, and it cannot be compared with a 90 under a different target. The five libraries differ in size, construct and design strategy, so a pooled table across targets would be meaningless and there is deliberately no way to build one.
  • An error bar beats a point estimate. Where a prediction carries an error estimate, as the melting temperature does, the error is the more informative of the two numbers. A wide error means the model has not seen anything resembling this sequence.
  • An empty cell is an empty cell. A metric that has not been computed is shown as pending, never filled with a default, an average or a zero.

The weak axis, stated up front

Published benchmarks show that co-folding confidence does not reliably distinguish a nanobody from its real partner from the same nanobody placed against something it has no reason to bind. The binding axis is therefore a prior, it is labelled as such wherever it appears, and the control set tab reports what a negative control did to it.

That control is the test that matters. Fifty sequences per target have their binding loops shuffled, which keeps every amino acid and changes only the order, and they are then run through exactly the same code as the real candidates. If a metric cannot separate a real candidate from its own shuffled twin, that metric is measuring two proteins placed near each other rather than two proteins binding, and it is reported as non-discriminating rather than quietly kept in the score.

The same logic bounds what the control can prove. Shuffling a loop leaves the amino acid composition untouched, so any metric computed from composition alone is identical between a candidate and its twin by construction. Those metrics are flagged in the code, and an axis built mostly from them is reported as untestable by this control rather than as having passed it.

GLP1R is scoped to the N-terminal extracellular domain only, by explicit decision. The seven-helix bundle that sits in the membrane and the loops around it are excluded, so a GLP1R result here cannot support any claim about binding the intact receptor in a cell. TNFa and TL1A are co-folded as three-part assemblies, because the site a nanobody usually binds spans the join between two subunits, and running them as single chains would be wrong rather than merely cheaper. The EGFR and TNFa libraries are enriched in histidine, which is a deliberate design choice intended to make binding weaken inside the acidic compartment a cell pulls the complex into.

Axis correlation, per target

The axes are not independent by construction. Hydrophobic patch area feeds non-specific binding, aggregation and expression at the same time, so two axes moving together may be one piece of evidence counted twice rather than two agreeing measurements. The correlation matrix is computed per target and any pair above 0.8 is stated here rather than left implicit.

What has been added since the first version

The first version scored binding from co-folding confidence alone. That is a single number from a single model, and the control set is what showed how little it carries on its own. Four additions since then, each one a separate piece of evidence about the same predicted complex:

  • Agreement between five predictions. Each complex is predicted five times from different starting noise. A candidate that lands somewhere different every time has told you nothing, however good the best of the five looks. See pose agreement.
  • How well the two surfaces fit. A shape complementarity statistic, which measures fit independently of how large the interface is, so a small neat interface is not punished for being small. See shape complementarity.
  • An inventory of the actual contacts. Hydrogen bonds, salt bridges and hydrophobic contacts are detected individually, drawn in the viewer and listed in the drawer, and the hydrogen bond count is also reported per unit of buried area so that it is not simply restating interface size. See hydrogen bond density.
  • Whether the binding loops are doing the binding. The share of contacts made by the CDRs rather than by the conserved scaffold. See CDR paratope.

Two changes to how the controls are run matter as much as the metrics themselves. Each shuffled control is now co-folded beside the candidate it was shuffled from, so the comparison is paired and the only difference inside a pair is the order of the loop residues. The earlier unpaired comparison was confounded: the co-folded candidates are the top ranks from Tier 1, and that rank is driven by surface hydrophobicity, so they differ from a random sample in ways that have nothing to do with shuffling. What that paired comparison settled was not the weights on the binding axis but what the axis is allowed to do: no component of it separated a real candidate from its own shuffled twin, and two of the seven separated them backwards, so the axis is shown and is not ranked on.

The shortlist is 24 per target, and 50 per target were co-folded with the intention that the 24 would be selected on the Tier 2 evidence rather than merely annotated with it. The controls did not support that. Because no binding metric beat its own shuffled twin, the 24 are the 24 the Tier 1 ranking gives, and the co-folds describe them rather than choose them: every binding number is computed, shown and excluded from both the Pareto front and the desirability ordering. This is a change from the plan, made on 2026-11-14, and the alternative was to let the shortlist turn on metrics the project's own negative control had just failed.

Two of the seven are worth naming, because they are reported in the table and a reader should know what the control said about them. Buried surface area and the predicted interface binding free energy both run the wrong way: pooled over the fifty pairs, the shuffled sequence buries more surface than the candidate it came from on 40 pairs of 50, and scores the better predicted binding energy on 37 of 50. The most likely reading is that a co-folding model will pack a shuffled loop into a large, comfortable looking interface, so those two measure how willingly a peptide is buried rather than whether a designed binder binds. They are shown with that caveat and they carry no weight.
